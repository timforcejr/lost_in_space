**How each item was checked.** Every row was looked up on 2026-11-15 against a primary record, not from memory:
ACL Anthology BibTeX export and abstract page (ACL venues), Crossref DOI metadata (IEEE, Springer, ACM, Nature, JAIR, Distill, Elsevier),
PMLR / NeurIPS / JMLR proceedings pages, and the arXiv API. Novelty claims were checked by reading the papers' text (PDF) where a claim depended on it.

**Status words.** VERIFIED = every field shown was confirmed on the primary record. CORRECTED = the citation as supplied (or the `.bib` entry) differs from the record; the change is stated. NOT CONFIRMED = could not be confirmed. A field that could not be confirmed inside an otherwise verified row is named in the status cell and repeated in section 4.

**Totals.** 53 citations in the two requested groups: 47 VERIFIED, 6 CORRECTED, 0 NOT CONFIRMED as a whole. Five single fields could not be confirmed (section 4). Fourteen further related-work citations (section 2) were all verified.

### 2.3 The three questions

**(a) Does existing work decompose mean-pooled cosine similarity exactly into token pairs? Yes.**

- Lee et al. (ACL 2022), Section 3.1.1, p. 5971, state it in closed form. With `s = (1/L) Σ x_i`, they write
  `1 − cos(s¹, s²) = 1 − Σ_i Σ_j [ (1/(L₁L₂)) · ‖x¹_i‖‖x²_j‖ / (‖s¹‖‖s²‖) ] · cos(x¹_i, x²_j)`.
  The bracket is their Eq. 3 "transportation matrix". This is the same identity as the project's word-pair decomposition.
- They also criticize it: the weight matrix has rank one, every token pair gets non-zero weight, and its interpretation agreed less with human judgment than their alternative.
- Vasileiou & Eberle (2024) use interactions computed "directly from token embeddings" as a baseline ("Embed."). It was not selective for part of speech on their synthetic task. In their perturbation test on English STSb and BIOSSES it scored close to the random baseline (for example BERT on STSb: random 10.28, Embed. 10.86, BiLRP 7.80; lower is better). Their text does not say exactly how the baseline is computed, so it may not be identical to the project's decomposition.
- Consequence for the write-up: do not claim the decomposition as new. Cite Lee et al. (2022) for the identity. State the limit plainly: it is exact over last-layer contextual token vectors, so it credits contextualised tokens, not input words. The project's own deletion test is the right check of whether that matters.
- Not found: a paper that presents this identity as an interactive explanation, or that tests it by deletion on a multilingual model. Three searches on this point, plus a full-text check of Lee et al., Yokoi et al. and Vasileiou & Eberle.

**(b) Does existing work run attribution for sentence similarity fully client-side in a browser? Not found in 4 searches.**

- Found, but different: Embedding Projector (interactive projection, no attribution); Transformer Explainer (GPT-2 running in the browser, no attribution, no similarity); several transformers.js demos that compute embeddings and cosine similarity in the browser (semantic search, pooling comparisons) with no attribution; LIT and exBERT (interactive tools with a browser interface, covered in 1B); XPLAINSIM (similarity attribution, but a Python package).
- The search results did not surface any project that combines in-browser inference, gradient or occlusion attribution, and sentence similarity. GitHub was searched only through web search, not through its own code search, so an unpublished hobby project could exist.
- Safe wording: "we are not aware of an in-browser tool that attributes a sentence-similarity score", not "the first".

**(c) Does existing work benchmark faithfulness of similarity attributions across languages? Not found in 4 searches; two papers come close.**

- Vasileiou & Eberle (2024) run a perturbation faithfulness test on similarity attributions, but only on English data (STSb, BIOSSES), although mBERT is one of the models. Their multilingual part (mSTSb: English, German, Russian, Chinese) reports correlation with human scores and a qualitative and part-of-speech analysis of English–German pairs. It has no per-language faithfulness numbers.
- Opitz et al. (2026) evaluate similarity attributions across ten English–X pairs (bg, da, es, et, hu, it, nl, pt, ru, sl), including this project's model, but against gold word alignments. That measures agreement with human alignment (plausibility), not deletion faithfulness. As read from their Table 2, `paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2` reaches F1 of about 0.51 (MaxAlign) and 0.63 (FlowAlign).
- Zaman & Belinkov (2022) and Zhao & Aletras (2024) measure attribution faithfulness across languages, but for classifiers (NLI and text classification), not for similarity models.
- The 2025 survey lists cross-lingual text similarity as an open direction.
- Not found: deletion-style (most-relevant-first versus least-relevant-first) faithfulness of similarity attributions reported per language, a weight-randomization sanity check for similarity attributions, or any such result for Hindi. Hindi is in none of the language sets above.
- Safe wording: the per-language deletion comparison on English, Spanish, Hindi and Chinese appears to be new as an experiment, but it is small-scale and extends Vasileiou & Eberle's test, so present it as an extension.
